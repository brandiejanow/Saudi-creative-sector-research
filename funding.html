Working paper 01 / Research dossier / 2025 archival sources

Financial Infrastructure
and Creative Careers

An examination of the questions recorded in the author's 2025 Saudi creative economy funding proposals, alongside official information about the existence and scale of financial programs.

Brandie Janow   |   Research begun in 2019   |   Source commentary, version 01 / October 2026

Abstract

Access requires more than the existence of a fund.

Two research proposals prepared in 2025 documented concerns about financial access, eligibility rules, independent creative practice, regional differences, weak private investment, and the instability of support based on single projects. The proposals set out questions and methods for a study that would examine how funding arrangements operate in practice. They are important primary documents for the author's research history, but they are not findings from the interviews, survey, national funding map, or regional workshops they proposed. [P01] [P02]

This dossier sets the original questions beside publicly reported financial programs and figures. A Cultural Development Fund financing program was announced in August 2024, and the Fund later reported cumulative project support. These sources demonstrate institutional activity. They do not directly establish who applied, who was unsuccessful, what obstacles remained, or the longer-term survival of supported creative businesses. [S05] [S04]

1 / Historical setting

The question recorded in 2025.

The longer research record contains discussions of design and creative sector conditions from 2019. By 2025, several concerns had been expressed as a formal research proposal focused on how creative professionals finance their activities and manage continuing costs.

The first document, Mapping the Financial Infrastructure of Saudi Arabia’s Creative Economy: Access, Barriers, and Long-Term Viability, is marked Draft 01 and prepared by Brandie Janow. Its problem statement describes uncertain funding pathways, eligibility requirements, legal and professional classification, and limited support for preparing financial applications. [P01]

The second document, Mapping the Creative Economy in Saudi Arabia: Infrastructure Gaps, Financial Barriers, and National Inclusion, broadens the inquiry to legal support, production tools, business services, information, and the needs of professionals outside established institutional networks. [P02]

“the invisible infrastructure behind creative practices”

Brandie Janow, 2025, Mapping the Creative Economy in Saudi Arabia, research abstract. [P02]

This phrase reflects the question running through the wider study. The presence of a cultural event or funding announcement says little about the ease of using the systems behind it. The gap between provision and actual access needs separate evidence.

3 / Proposed study design

Research planned is not research completed.

One related 2025 submission, Building a Sustainable Funding System for Saudi Arabia’s Creative Economy, sets out four main question groups: funding mechanisms and access; regulation and legal classification; private investment; and capability and long-term continuity. It proposes a minimum of 30 interviews, quantitative surveys, policy and legal review, regional workshops, case mapping, and prototype testing. [P03]

The figure of 30 is a planned interview target, not a count of completed interviews. Likewise, references to a national funding map and recommendations describe proposed outputs. They must not be represented as delivered research without dated files confirming completion.

There are two separate research tasks here. The first is a historical study of what the author was identifying and proposing in 2025. The second is an empirical study of how funding actually operated, with applicant-level and institutional evidence. The first can be documented from the drafts; the second requires additional sources.

4 / Institutional comparison

A financing program exists. How was it used?

In August 2024, the Cultural Development Fund announced a cultural financing program designed for micro, small, and medium cultural enterprises across 16 cultural sub-sectors. That announcement provides evidence of a financing route being introduced before the author's 2025 proposal. [S05]

In April 2026, the Fund reported that it had provided more than SAR 770 million of financial support to 165 cultural projects since its establishment, alongside developmental services benefiting more than 1,630 creative individuals and entrepreneurs. These figures describe the Fund's reported activity through the first quarter of 2026. [S04]

165Cultural projects reported as receiving cumulative Fund support through Q1 2026. [S04]
1,630+People reported as benefiting from developmental services, a different measure from funded projects. [S04]

The published figures cannot answer the author's original access questions on their own. They do not provide a comparable denominator of eligible creative enterprises, unsuccessful applicants, independent workers, or regional outcomes. Nor do they establish that a particular grant, loan, or service caused a later business to remain active.

An appropriate comparison would bring together the criteria of each funding route, the number and characteristics of applicants, approval rates, terms, applicant costs, and follow-up outcomes. Only then could the publication judge whether specific barriers persisted or changed.

5 / Analytical distinction

Availability, accessibility, and sustained use.

Three questions must remain separate. First, did a financing or support mechanism exist during the period studied? Second, could a defined group of creative professionals discover, qualify for, and use it? Third, what happened to their practice after receiving or failing to receive that support?

An institutional announcement may establish the first condition. Application records and interviews can help investigate the second. Repeated observations and follow-up outcomes are needed for the third. Treating the existence of a program as proof of its effectiveness collapses different questions into one conclusion.

This distinction does not assume that public support failed. It requires the same level of evidence to demonstrate success, exclusion, continuation, or a change in conditions.

6 / Current position

A documented research question, not a national verdict.

The author's 2025 proposals establish that financial routes, eligibility, regional access, private capital, legal classification, and independent creative practice were being identified as subjects requiring systematic study. Public sources establish that new financing programs were being developed and that substantial institutional support was later reported.

Neither source class, on its own, resolves whether the people described in the proposals could gain access or remain active in the sector. The next task is to link individual claims to documents, periods, populations, and follow-up evidence, and to publish both confirmation and counterexamples.
